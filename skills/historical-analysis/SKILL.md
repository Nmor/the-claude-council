# Historical Analysis

## Purpose

Historical analysis is the disciplined reconstruction of past events, structures, mentalities, and contingencies from imperfect, partial, and biased evidence. It is not chronicle (a list of what happened) and not myth (a story we tell ourselves about origins). It is a rigorous craft governed by source criticism, contextual reading, transparent argument, and explicit awareness of the historian's own position relative to the past.

This skill exists because most "historical" reasoning that appears in business, policy, technology, and engineering contexts is bad history. Common failure modes: cherry-picking analogies ("this is the Munich moment"), reading the present into the past (presentism), treating the past as inevitable march toward our world (Whig history), citing secondary syntheses as if they were evidence, ignoring provenance and chain of custody, mistaking digitised sources for originals, and using historical narrative to ratify a conclusion that was reached on other grounds. Good historical analysis treats the past as a foreign country (L. P. Hartley), interrogates every source, holds counterfactuals open, and presents argument with the evidence visible.

What this skill does NOT do: it does not produce predictions of the future (history is not predictive); it does not provide moral lessons (the past is not a Sunday school); it does not adjudicate present political disputes (though it can illuminate them); it does not equate the historian's interpretation with what "really happened" (all historical writing is interpretation grounded in evidence).

## When to Fire

Engage historical-analysis when work involves:

- Citing precedent ("we did this in 2018 and it failed") — verify the analogy holds
- Drawing lessons from a prior incident, project, deployment, regime, or era
- Writing post-mortems, retrospectives, or institutional histories
- Reasoning about long-run change (architectural evolution, regulatory drift, market formation)
- Producing public-facing historical content (anniversaries, founding stories, brand heritage)
- Building or maintaining archives (organisational, technical, documentary)
- Conducting oral history (interviews with founders, retirees, witnesses)
- Citing historical figures, events, or texts in argument
- Building counterfactual analysis ("what if we had chosen the other architecture")
- Conducting longitudinal research that crosses regime change, organisational restructure, or technology shifts
- Any claim about cause and effect across decades

Do NOT engage when:

- The question is about current state (use observability, not history)
- The question is genuinely predictive (history is not a forecasting tool)
- "History" is being used rhetorically to ratify a present decision (call this out)

## Core Patterns

### 1. Source typology + source criticism

Every historical claim rests on sources, and every source has provenance, purpose, and bias. Marc Bloch's *Historian's Craft* establishes the basic taxonomy: **intentional sources** (memoirs, chronicles, propaganda — written *for* posterity) and **unintentional sources** (court records, account books, archaeological remains, private letters — created for immediate purposes and surviving accidentally). Unintentional sources are generally more trustworthy on the facts they incidentally record (a tax assessment is not lying about a farmer's property; a court record is not embellishing the price of bread) — though they reflect the categories and concerns of the recording institution.

Source criticism — the Quellenkritik tradition formalised by Leopold von Ranke and developed by Bloch — asks of every source:

- **Authenticity**: Is this what it claims to be? (Forgery test — material, handwriting, watermark, content anachronism. The Donation of Constantine, exposed by Valla in 1440 via Latin philology, is the canonical case.)
- **Integrity**: Has it been altered since creation? Censorship, redaction, later interpolation, mistranscription
- **Provenance**: Chain of custody from creation to present access. Who held it? Who selected it for preservation? What got destroyed?
- **Authorship + position**: Who wrote it? In what role? With what knowledge, what biases, what interests, what audience?
- **Date + context**: When? Where? In response to what? Within what genre conventions?
- **Bias of recorder**: Every record reflects the categories of the recording authority. A medieval bishop's register reflects what the bishop's office cared about, not what villagers cared about.

Apply to every source — primary archival document, contemporary newspaper, later memoir, secondary historian's narrative, oral testimony, digitised image. Sources do not "speak for themselves"; they answer the questions a historian asks, and the questions presuppose interpretive frames.

### 2. Primary, secondary, tertiary — and the "primary" trap

The conventional hierarchy (per research-methods skill):

- **Primary source**: produced within the period under study by participants or contemporary witnesses — letters, diaries, government records, newspapers of the time, photographs, artifacts, oral testimony from participants
- **Secondary source**: scholarly interpretation drawing on primary sources — journal articles, monographs by historians
- **Tertiary source**: synthesis of secondary literature — textbooks, encyclopedias, popular surveys

The trap: "primary" is relative to the question. A 1965 government memo is primary for a study of 1965 policy; it is secondary for a study of the events of 1945 that it summarises. A historian's 1920 monograph is secondary if you study the events; it is primary if you study the historiography of the 1920s. Always identify what your source is primary FOR.

Additional trap: digitised primary sources are not equivalent to originals without chain-of-custody verification. The digitisation introduces selection (what got scanned), framing (image crop, resolution), and metadata that may misrepresent the artifact. For consequential claims, consult the original or a verified facsimile with archivist annotation.

### 3. Archival methodology + ISAD(G)

Archives are not libraries. They are organised by provenance (the *fonds* — the records of one creating entity, kept together to preserve their organic relationships), not by subject. The principle is **respect des fonds** (Natalis de Wailly, 1841): keep records of one creator together; do not interfile by topic.

ISAD(G) 2nd edition defines the multi-level description hierarchy:

| Level | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Fonds** | "Records of the Acme Corporation, 1923-1998" |
| **Sub-fonds** | "Acme Corporation, Engineering Division records, 1947-1998" |
| **Series** | "Engineering project files, 1947-1998" |
| **Sub-series** | "Engineering project files, mainframe era, 1947-1972" |
| **File** | "Project Falcon design files, 1968-1971" |
| **Item** | "Falcon system architecture diagram, 14 March 1969" |

Finding aids (now usually EAD3-encoded) describe at multiple levels. Researchers navigate top-down: identify the fonds, narrow to series, request specific files. Skipping the hierarchy ("just give me anything on X") misses the structural context that gives individual documents meaning.

Engineering equivalents: a service's `audits/` directory, a project's `docs/adr/` folder, a repository's commit history are *fonds* — organised by creator, preserving organic relationships, requiring multi-level navigation.

### 4. Periodisation as interpretive choice

Periodisation — dividing the past into named eras — is interpretive, not natural. "The Renaissance," "the Industrial Revolution," "the Cold War," "the dot-com era" are constructs that organise narrative; they privilege some causes and obscure others. Common periodisation pitfalls:

- **Backward projection**: naming a period from its outcome ("the road to war"). The actors did not know what they were on the road to
- **Centre-of-the-world periodisation**: dating world events from Western European chronology ignores Chinese, Islamic, Indian, African parallel chronologies
- **Decadal thinking**: "the 1960s" as a coherent unit. The cultural 1960s in the US started ~1963 and ended ~1974; calendar decades are administrative artifacts
- **Whig periodisation**: dividing history into "progress toward us" stages

Always disclose the periodisation choice and its alternatives. Different periodisations produce different stories.

### 5. The three temporalities (Braudel)

Fernand Braudel's *On History* (1969) divides historical time into three planes:

- **Longue durée** (long duration): geographic, climatic, structural — change over centuries. The Mediterranean basin, the role of money, the slow expansion of literacy
- **Conjoncture** (medium term): economic cycles, demographic curves, political regimes — change over decades
- **Événementielle** (event history): wars, elections, scandals, individual decisions — surface foam on the deeper tides

A historical question may live mainly at one plane but is shaped by the others. The 2008 financial crisis is an event; it sits on a conjuncture (financialisation since the 1970s); which sits on a longue durée (the development of credit instruments since the Renaissance). Single-plane analysis flattens the picture.

Engineering analogue: the same is true of architectural change. A specific bug fix is *événement*; the deprecation cycle of a framework is *conjoncture*; the long-run convergence on RESTful interfaces is *longue durée*.

### 6. Oral history methodology

Oral history is the systematic recording of individuals' recollections — not casual interviews but structured, archived, citable testimony. Per Ritchie + Thompson + Portelli:

**Pre-interview**: research the subject's biography, historical context, the events they witnessed. Prepare open-ended questions (not yes/no) organised chronologically or thematically. Obtain informed consent in writing (Oral History Association template) covering recording, transcription, deposit in an archive, future use, restrictions.

**Interview**: minimise interviewer voice; let the subject speak. Use "tell me about..." not "did you...". Note non-verbal cues, hesitations, emotional weight — these become part of the record. Cover sequential events but be prepared to follow associative memory.

**Post-interview**: transcribe verbatim including pauses, false starts, laughter. Allow the subject to review the transcript (some projects require this). Deposit in an archive with metadata (date, location, equipment, consent terms). Cite as: Speaker, interview by Interviewer, location, date, archive accession number.

Portelli's critical insight: oral history's "errors" (misdating, conflation, exaggeration) are themselves historical evidence — evidence of how memory works, how meaning is made retrospectively, what stories communities tell themselves. A factually wrong memory is not worthless data; it is data about the speaker's interpretive frame.

### 7. Cliometrics + quantitative history

Cliometrics applies econometric and statistical methods to historical questions: demographic history (parish registers), economic history (price series, trade volumes), prosopography (collective biography of named groups). Foundational works: Robert Fogel + Stanley Engerman *Time on the Cross* (1974, controversial), Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie *Montaillou* (1975) (microhistory + quantitative parish records).

Pitfalls — and the Reinhart-Rogoff 2009 cautionary tale: published data series may contain errors (the famous Excel spreadsheet error in *This Time Is Different*'s 90%-debt-to-GDP threshold, exposed by Herndon, Ash + Pollin 2013, changed the apparent finding from "growth collapses above 90%" to "growth modestly declines"). Always: cite data sources, publish data + code (FAIR principles per research-methods skill), enable replication. Quantitative history without published data is faith.

### 8. Counterfactual analysis (disciplined)

What if Napoleon had won at Waterloo? What if the patent had been filed three months earlier? Counterfactuals are unavoidable in historical reasoning (any causal claim implies a counterfactual — "X caused Y" means "without X, Y would not have occurred") but undisciplined counterfactuals become fantasy. Niall Ferguson's *Virtual History* (1997) rules:

- Counterfactuals must be **plausible at the moment of decision** — not retrofitted from later knowledge
- Counterfactuals must hold the wider context **constant** — change one variable, not the world
- Counterfactuals must rest on **evidence about what actors knew + considered** — what alternatives were on the table for them
- Counterfactuals are **interpretive tools for assessing causal weight**, not predictions of alternative pasts

Engineering: "if we had chosen Postgres instead of MongoDB in 2014" is a useful counterfactual only with evidence about the 2014 decision context, the alternatives genuinely available then, and the constraints binding at the time.

### 9. Historiography (knowing the conversation)

Every historical question sits within a historiography — the accumulated scholarly conversation about that question. Annales school (Bloch, Febvre, Braudel, Le Goff) emphasised structures + mentalités over events; Marxist historiography (Hobsbawm, E. P. Thompson) emphasised class + economic relations; cultural turn (Ginzburg, Natalie Davis, Robert Darnton) emphasised meaning + interpretation; subaltern studies (Guha, Spivak) emphasised perspectives erased by colonial archives; environmental history (Cronon, Crosby) emphasised ecology + climate as historical actors.

Before writing on a topic, read the historiographical state of the question (current review essays in *American Historical Review*, *Past & Present*, *Journal of Modern History*, regional equivalents). Citing a 1958 monograph as if it were current scholarship is citation laundering.

### 10. Citation discipline (Chicago notes-bibliography)

Humanities citation conventions differ from STEM. Chicago Manual of Style 17th edition (notes-bibliography style) is standard for history:

**Footnote** (full first citation):
> Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft*, trans. Peter Putnam (New York: Vintage, 1992), 64-66.

**Footnote** (short subsequent):
> Bloch, *Historian's Craft*, 71.

**Bibliography**:
> Bloch, Marc. *The Historian's Craft*. Translated by Peter Putnam. New York: Vintage, 1992.

**Archival citation**:
> Letter from Smith to Jones, 14 March 1969, Box 7, Folder 3, Acme Corporation Records (MS 423), Special Collections, University Library.

Include archive name, collection name + ID, box/folder, date, document description. Without this, no reader can verify the source. Many history journals reject articles whose archival citations cannot be located.

## Anti-Patterns

**Anti-pattern: Presentism** — reading the past through present categories and judging actors by present standards. Historical actors did not have access to our concepts (the medieval peasant did not think of herself as "feudal"; the 1860 American did not understand "race" the way 2026 sociologists do). Reconstruct categories from the sources, not from contemporary common sense.

**Anti-pattern: Whig history** — Herbert Butterfield's 1931 critique of histories that treat the past as inevitable progress toward present arrangements (parliamentary democracy, market capitalism, scientific rationality, our specific stack). Whig history selects evidence to confirm the arrival; it ignores roads not taken, costs paid, alternatives suppressed.

**Anti-pattern: Anachronism** — using terms, concepts, or technologies that did not exist in the period. "Industrial policy" applied to the seventeenth century, "user experience" applied to 1980s software, "agile" applied to 1960s engineering. Define terms in period categories or flag the anachronism explicitly.

**Anti-pattern: Single-source reliance** — building a major claim on one document, one witness, one chronicle. Triangulate: corroborate with independent sources of different types (per research-methods triangulation pattern). When triangulation is impossible (unique source), explicitly note the limitation.

**Anti-pattern: Hindsight bias** — describing past decisions as obvious because we know the outcome. The 2008 mortgage crisis "should have been seen" — but it wasn't, by most well-positioned observers, and reconstructing the information environment of 2005 requires reading what 2005 analysts wrote, not what 2010 analysts wrote about 2005.

**Anti-pattern: Confirmation bias in historical narrative** — selecting evidence to support a thesis already adopted. The discipline of historiography demands engagement with the strongest opposing evidence. Present the counter-case and argue why your reading is stronger.

**Anti-pattern: Citation laundering** — citing a tertiary source (Wikipedia, popular history book, marketing copy) as if it were primary research. Read the primary source the tertiary work cites, OR cite the tertiary work explicitly as a tertiary survey, not as evidence.

**Anti-pattern: Ignoring provenance** — using a document without asking who held it, who selected it for preservation, what got destroyed. Survivor bias in archives is severe: court documents survive because courts kept them; the illiterate's voice rarely survives because nobody wrote it down. The archive is not the past; it is what powerful institutions chose to preserve.

**Anti-pattern: Digitised-as-original** — treating a JSTOR scan, an Internet Archive image, or a database transcription as equivalent to the original. Scans crop, transcriptions err, OCR misreads. For consequential claims verify against the original or annotated facsimile.

**Anti-pattern: Counterfactual without evidence** — "if the Confederacy had won the Civil War" extended into a baroque alternative timeline. Counterfactuals are interpretive tools for assessing causal weight, bounded by evidence about the actual decision context. Speculation without evidence is fiction, useful perhaps as fiction, not as history.

**Anti-pattern: Treating oral history as either flawless or worthless** — both errors. Oral testimony is evidence — about events, about memory, about meaning. It corroborates and complicates documentary evidence; it does not replace it; its "errors" are themselves data per Portelli.

**Anti-pattern: Conflating chronicle with analysis** — listing what happened in order is not history. History asks why, how, with what consequences, in what relation to structure, for whom. A timeline is the start of the work, not its product.

**Anti-pattern: Reading the past for present validation** — "the founders agreed with my position." The past is a foreign country; it does not exist to ratify present preferences. When the past disagrees with us, that disagreement is data worth understanding.

**Anti-pattern: Ignoring historiography** — writing on a topic without engaging the prior scholarly conversation. The result is reinvention at best, error at worst (you repeat a thesis disproved in 1987).

## Verification Checklist

- [ ] **Source typology classified**: every cited source identified as primary / secondary / tertiary, relative to the specific question
- [ ] **Source criticism performed**: authenticity, integrity, provenance, authorship, position, date, bias documented for every consequential source
- [ ] **Archival citations complete**: archive name, collection, box, folder, date, document description per Chicago 17th
- [ ] **Periodisation declared**: chosen period boundaries named + justified; alternative periodisations acknowledged
- [ ] **Temporal scale specified**: longue durée / conjoncture / événement frame identified; cross-scale dependencies noted
- [ ] **Historiography reviewed**: current scholarly conversation on the question summarised; key positions named
- [ ] **Counterfactuals (if used) disciplined**: bounded by evidence about decision context; one variable changed; plausible at the moment
- [ ] **Triangulation attempted**: claim corroborated by independent sources of different types; single-source limitations flagged
- [ ] **Provenance + survival bias acknowledged**: what got preserved + why; what was destroyed + suppressed; whose voices are absent
- [ ] **Period categories used**: terms defined in the actors' own conceptual vocabulary; anachronisms flagged
- [ ] **Quantitative data published**: data series + processing code available; spreadsheet errors checkable (Reinhart-Rogoff lesson)
- [ ] **Oral history consent + archiving**: informed consent obtained, transcript reviewed, deposit + accession recorded
- [ ] **Counter-evidence engaged**: strongest opposing reading presented + argued against
- [ ] **Presentism check**: have I judged the past by present standards? Reconstructed past categories?
- [ ] **Whig check**: have I treated the present as the inevitable arrival? Acknowledged roads not taken?
- [ ] **Hindsight check**: am I describing decisions as obvious because I know the outcome?
- [ ] **Citation discipline**: Chicago 17th notes-bibliography (or appropriate equivalent) consistently applied
- [ ] **Original consulted**: for consequential claims, original or verified facsimile examined, not just digitised reproduction

## Why This Skill Exists

Bad historical reasoning permeates technical and business contexts in identifiable patterns:

- **Folk history within organisations** — "we tried microservices in 2018 and it failed" with no document trail; the speaker was not there; the failure was actually a specific service's deployment misconfiguration, not microservices as architecture. Decisions get made on the folk story
- **Whig narratives of technology** — the inevitable march from monolith to SOA to microservices to serverless to whatever-is-next; this conceals the actual choices and trade-offs at each step and treats current architecture as the destination
- **Founding myths** — "we built this company to..." retrofitted to current strategy; the founding documents say something else; the difference is not embarrassment to suppress, it is data about institutional change
- **Cherry-picked analogies** — "this is the Munich moment" or "the iPhone moment" or "the Eastman Kodak moment" — invoked to short-circuit argument; the specific historical context that made Munich Munich is usually absent from the analogy
- **Anniversary content as marketing** — "ten years ago we shipped X" presented as history when it is brand promotion; the actual ten-years-ago archive often tells a different, messier story
- **Presentism in post-mortems** — "in retrospect we should have known"; in retrospect we always should have known; the question is what the available evidence + decision context supported at the time
- **Citation laundering** — "as the Romans knew" repeated from a Twitter quote that came from a self-help book that paraphrased a 19th-century historian who got it from Cicero (probably) but with an interpretation Cicero would not have recognised

Disciplined historical analysis rejects all of these. It treats the past as a foreign country, demands evidence with provenance, holds counterfactuals open, presents argument with evidence visible, and refuses to retrofit narratives to present preferences. The cost is more work and less clean stories. The benefit is interpretations that survive contact with the archive — and decisions, post-mortems, and strategies that engage with what actually happened rather than with what we wish had happened.

The line from L. P. Hartley, used by David Lowenthal as the title of his 1985 study of historical consciousness, holds: the past is a foreign country; they do things differently there. Engineering organisations, startups, and policy bodies that pretend otherwise — that read the past as a mirror — repeat its mistakes while believing they are learning from them.

## Learning hooks

Per `~/.claude/rules/common/continuous-learning-mandate.md`:

**Signals to watch**:

- Post-mortem reads founder / engineer intent into events without contemporaneous evidence (presentism)
- Selection bias in archive — only surviving artifacts cited, lost decisions invisible (Whig history)
- Single source treated as authoritative without corroboration (oral-history confabulation, founding-myth canonisation)
- Anachronistic categories applied to pre-existence-of-the-category state (e.g., "SRE practices" before 2003)
- Hindsight bias inflating probability of outcomes that became obvious only after the fact
- Counterfactuals collapsed to a single "what would have happened" line (deterministic reading of contingent choices)
- Citation laundering — Twitter / blog / self-help chain substituted for primary source
- Periodisation imposed on continuous change (artificial era boundaries hiding actual evolution)

**Refinement candidates**:

- New archival-discipline pattern when a recurring evidence class (Slack archive, GitHub PR history, ADR trail) needs codified treatment
- Counterfactual-framing template when post-mortems repeatedly collapse to single-narrative readings
- Bias-identification addition when a recurring fallacy (e.g., founder hagiography, technology determinism) surfaces in reviews
- Cliometrics template when quantitative historical claims (e.g., "burn rate trended X") need rigorous time-series treatment
