## Anti-Patterns

**Anti-pattern: Presentism** — reading the past through present categories and judging actors by
present standards. Historical actors did not have access to our concepts (the medieval peasant did
not think of herself as "feudal"; the 1860 American did not understand "race" the way 2026
sociologists do). Reconstruct categories from the sources, not from contemporary common sense.

**Anti-pattern: Whig history** — Herbert Butterfield's 1931 critique of histories that treat the
past as inevitable progress toward present arrangements (parliamentary democracy, market capitalism,
scientific rationality, our specific stack). Whig history selects evidence to confirm the arrival;
it ignores roads not taken, costs paid, alternatives suppressed.

**Anti-pattern: Anachronism** — using terms, concepts, or technologies that did not exist in the
period. "Industrial policy" applied to the seventeenth century, "user experience" applied to 1980s
software, "agile" applied to 1960s engineering. Define terms in period categories or flag the
anachronism explicitly.

**Anti-pattern: Single-source reliance** — building a major claim on one document, one witness, one
chronicle. Triangulate: corroborate with independent sources of different types (per
research-methods triangulation pattern). When triangulation is impossible (unique source),
explicitly note the limitation.

**Anti-pattern: Hindsight bias** — describing past decisions as obvious because we know the outcome.
The 2008 mortgage crisis "should have been seen" — but it wasn't, by most well-positioned observers,
and reconstructing the information environment of 2005 requires reading what 2005 analysts wrote,
not what 2010 analysts wrote about 2005.

**Anti-pattern: Confirmation bias in historical narrative** — selecting evidence to support a thesis
already adopted. The discipline of historiography demands engagement with the strongest opposing
evidence. Present the counter-case and argue why your reading is stronger.

**Anti-pattern: Citation laundering** — citing a tertiary source (Wikipedia, popular history book,
marketing copy) as if it were primary research. Read the primary source the tertiary work cites, OR
cite the tertiary work explicitly as a tertiary survey, not as evidence.

**Anti-pattern: Ignoring provenance** — using a document without asking who held it, who selected it
for preservation, what got destroyed. Survivor bias in archives is severe: court documents survive
because courts kept them; the illiterate's voice rarely survives because nobody wrote it down. The
archive is not the past; it is what powerful institutions chose to preserve.

**Anti-pattern: Digitised-as-original** — treating a JSTOR scan, an Internet Archive image, or a
database transcription as equivalent to the original. Scans crop, transcriptions err, OCR misreads.
For consequential claims verify against the original or annotated facsimile.

**Anti-pattern: Counterfactual without evidence** — "if the Confederacy had won the Civil War"
extended into a baroque alternative timeline. Counterfactuals are interpretive tools for assessing
causal weight, bounded by evidence about the actual decision context. Speculation without evidence
is fiction, useful perhaps as fiction, not as history.

**Anti-pattern: Treating oral history as either flawless or worthless** — both errors. Oral
testimony is evidence — about events, about memory, about meaning. It corroborates and complicates
documentary evidence; it does not replace it; its "errors" are themselves data per Portelli.

**Anti-pattern: Conflating chronicle with analysis** — listing what happened in order is not
history. History asks why, how, with what consequences, in what relation to structure, for whom. A
timeline is the start of the work, not its product.

**Anti-pattern: Reading the past for present validation** — "the founders agreed with my position."
The past is a foreign country; it does not exist to ratify present preferences. When the past
disagrees with us, that disagreement is data worth understanding.

**Anti-pattern: Ignoring historiography** — writing on a topic without engaging the prior scholarly
conversation. The result is reinvention at best, error at worst (you repeat a thesis disproved in
1987).
