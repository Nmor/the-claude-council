## 9. Historiography (knowing the conversation)

Every historical question sits within a historiography — the accumulated scholarly conversation
about that question. Annales school (Bloch, Febvre, Braudel, Le Goff) emphasised structures +
mentalités over events; Marxist historiography (Hobsbawm, E. P. Thompson) emphasised class +
economic relations; cultural turn (Ginzburg, Natalie Davis, Robert Darnton) emphasised meaning +
interpretation; subaltern studies (Guha, Spivak) emphasised perspectives erased by colonial
archives; environmental history (Cronon, Crosby) emphasised ecology + climate as historical actors.

Before writing on a topic, read the historiographical state of the question (current review essays
in *American Historical Review*, *Past & Present*, *Journal of Modern History*, regional
equivalents). Citing a 1958 monograph as if it were current scholarship is citation laundering.

## 10. Citation discipline (Chicago notes-bibliography)

Humanities citation conventions differ from STEM. Chicago Manual of Style 17th edition
(notes-bibliography style) is standard for history:

**Footnote** (full first citation):
> Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft*, trans. Peter Putnam (New York: Vintage, 1992), 64-66.

**Footnote** (short subsequent):
> Bloch, *Historian's Craft*, 71.

**Bibliography**:
> Bloch, Marc. *The Historian's Craft*. Translated by Peter Putnam. New York: Vintage, 1992.

**Archival citation**:
> Letter from Smith to Jones, 14 March 1969, Box 7, Folder 3, Acme Corporation Records (MS 423),
> Special Collections, University Library.

Include archive name, collection name + ID, box/folder, date, document description. Without this, no
reader can verify the source. Many history journals reject articles whose archival citations cannot
be located.
