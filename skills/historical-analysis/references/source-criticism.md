## 1. Source typology + source criticism

Every historical claim rests on sources, and every source has provenance, purpose, and bias. Marc
Bloch's *Historian's Craft* establishes the basic taxonomy: **intentional sources** (memoirs,
chronicles, propaganda — written *for* posterity) and **unintentional sources** (court records,
account books, archaeological remains, private letters — created for immediate purposes and
surviving accidentally). Unintentional sources are generally more trustworthy on the facts they
incidentally record (a tax assessment is not lying about a farmer's property; a court record is not
embellishing the price of bread) — though they reflect the categories and concerns of the recording
institution.

Source criticism — the Quellenkritik tradition formalised by Leopold von Ranke and developed by
Bloch — asks of every source:

- **Authenticity**: Is this what it claims to be? (Forgery test — material, handwriting, watermark,
  content anachronism. The Donation of Constantine, exposed by Valla in 1440 via Latin philology, is
  the canonical case.)
- **Integrity**: Has it been altered since creation? Censorship, redaction, later interpolation,
  mistranscription
- **Provenance**: Chain of custody from creation to present access. Who held it? Who selected it for
  preservation? What got destroyed?
- **Authorship + position**: Who wrote it? In what role? With what knowledge, what biases, what
  interests, what audience?
- **Date + context**: When? Where? In response to what? Within what genre conventions?
- **Bias of recorder**: Every record reflects the categories of the recording authority. A medieval
  bishop's register reflects what the bishop's office cared about, not what villagers cared about.

Apply to every source — primary archival document, contemporary newspaper, later memoir, secondary
historian's narrative, oral testimony, digitised image. Sources do not "speak for themselves"; they
answer the questions a historian asks, and the questions presuppose interpretive frames.

## 2. Primary, secondary, tertiary — and the "primary" trap

The conventional hierarchy (per research-methods skill):

- **Primary source**: produced within the period under study by participants or contemporary
  witnesses — letters, diaries, government records, newspapers of the time, photographs, artifacts,
  oral testimony from participants
- **Secondary source**: scholarly interpretation drawing on primary sources — journal articles,
  monographs by historians
- **Tertiary source**: synthesis of secondary literature — textbooks, encyclopedias, popular surveys

The trap: "primary" is relative to the question. A 1965 government memo is primary for a study of
1965 policy; it is secondary for a study of the events of 1945 that it summarises. A historian's
1920 monograph is secondary if you study the events; it is primary if you study the historiography
of the 1920s. Always identify what your source is primary FOR.

Additional trap: digitised primary sources are not equivalent to originals without chain-of-custody
verification. The digitisation introduces selection (what got scanned), framing (image crop,
resolution), and metadata that may misrepresent the artifact. For consequential claims, consult the
original or a verified facsimile with archivist annotation.
