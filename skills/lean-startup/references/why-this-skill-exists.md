## Why This Skill Exists

Most product failures aren't execution failures — they're
**building-the-wrong-thing failures**. The most disciplined
engineering team will fail if it builds a thing nobody wants.

Lean Startup attacks this failure mode directly. Its central
claim — that startups exist under conditions of extreme
uncertainty and require a methodology DIFFERENT from
established-business management — is empirically validated by
the venture-backed startup graveyard.

Common Lean Startup misreadings that this skill explicitly
counters:

1. **"MVP = ship something cheap and fast"** — wrong. MVP =
   smallest experiment that produces VALIDATED LEARNING. Often
   it's a landing page or a manual workflow.
2. **"Pivoting is failing"** — wrong. Pivoting is the
   productivity of the search. Not pivoting when the data
   demands it is the failure.
3. **"We'll measure success once we launch"** — wrong. The
   measurement plan is part of the experiment design BEFORE
   the build.
4. **"Get to product-market fit, then scale"** — correct, but
   the "then scale" part is widely misexecuted. Hiring sales
   - marketing teams before PMF is one of the most expensive
   mistakes in startup history.
5. **"Lean Startup is for software startups"** — wrong. Ries
   explicitly addresses enterprise innovation (The Startup
   Way), hardware (Pebble, Glowforge), and even government
   (UK Government Digital Service, US 18F).

Real-world signals this skill should fire:

- A team is building a thing for 6 months without talking to a
  single customer
- A founder is convinced "users want this" but can't show one
  who'll pay
- A product is launched and "didn't catch fire" — the team
  doesn't know whether to iterate or kill it
- A B2B team is scaling sales hires before the first sales
  motion is repeatable
- An OKR document lists shipped features as Key Results
- "We're pivoting" is said without naming what was falsified
- Cohort retention is never mentioned in product review
- The roadmap is "features customers asked for" with no
  prioritisation by riskiest assumption

In each case, the principal-level response is to slow down,
identify the riskiest assumption, design the smallest test,
and run it BEFORE more capital is committed. That's the entire
purpose of this skill.

Standards cited grounding (verbatim, with editions):

- Ries, *The Lean Startup* (Currency, 2011) — the canonical
  source.
- Ries, *The Startup Way* (Currency, 2017) — enterprise
  application.
- Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (K&S Ranch, 2005,
  rev 2013) — Customer Development origin.
- Blank + Dorf, *The Startup Owner's Manual* (K&S Ranch,
  2012) — operational playbook.
- Osterwalder + Pigneur, *Business Model Generation* (Wiley,
  2010) — Business Model Canvas.
- Osterwalder et al., *Value Proposition Design* (Wiley,
  2014) — Value Proposition Canvas.
- Maurya, *Running Lean* (O'Reilly, 3rd ed 2022) — Lean
  Canvas.
- Cagan, *Inspired* (Wiley, 2nd ed 2017) — dual-track agile.
- Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits* (Product Talk, 2021)
  — modern discovery cadence.
- Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap* (O'Reilly, 2018) — outcome
  vs output.
- Ellis + Brown, *Hacking Growth* (Currency, 2017) — post-PMF
  growth.
- Kohavi, Tang, Xu, *Trustworthy Online Controlled
  Experiments* (Cambridge, 2020) — A/B testing rigour.

These are the principal-level references a Lean Startup
practitioner uses to ground decisions in evidence rather than
opinion.
