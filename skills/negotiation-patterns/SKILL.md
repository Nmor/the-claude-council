## Purpose

Negotiation is the process by which parties with differing interests
reach agreements without resorting to dictation or warfare. Every
non-trivial professional decision — what to build, who to hire, who
gets credit, what a contract says, what a vendor charges, where the
data centre lives — passes through some form of negotiation.

Most people negotiate badly because they conflate negotiation with
**bargaining over a single number** (price). Principal-level
negotiation is structurally different: it identifies the underlying
**interests** behind stated positions, discovers value-creating
**tradeoffs** across multiple issues, sets explicit **walkaway
points** before entering the room, and uses **tactical empathy** to
keep counterparties engaged through difficult conversations.

This skill provides:

1. Interest-based (integrative) negotiation — the Harvard Method
2. BATNA / ZOPA / reservation value preparation discipline
3. The two dimensions: claiming value vs creating value
4. Multi-issue tradeoff design + scoring systems
5. Tactical empathy + the Chris Voss methodology
6. Anchoring + reciprocity + commitment + framing — and when each backfires
7. Cross-cultural negotiation patterns (Hofstede, Brett)
8. Negotiation ethics + what's never acceptable
9. Common manipulation tactics and how to neutralise them
10. Internal-vs-external negotiation differences

It is NOT a script for "winning" — at the principal level, the goal
is durable value-creating agreements that both sides perform on.

## Core Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Two Dimensions — Creating + Claiming Value

Every negotiation has two simultaneous dynamics (Lax + Sebenius
1986):

1. **Creating value (integrative)** — finding tradeoffs across
   multiple issues so the joint outcome exceeds simple
   split-the-difference; the proverbial "expanding the pie"
2. **Claiming value (distributive)** — capturing as much of the
   value as possible for your side

These ARE in tension: aggressive claiming (extreme first offers,
threats, deception) signals competition and shuts down the
information sharing that creating value requires. Disclosing your
interests transparently invites the counterparty to share theirs
— but also lets them claim more.

The principal-level skill is calibrating: create enough value to
make the deal worth doing for both sides, then claim a fair share
of the surplus. Pure creators leave money on the table; pure
claimers either kill deals or build agreements that the
counterparty resents and underperforms.

### Pattern 2: BATNA, Reservation Value, ZOPA — the Preparation Triad

The single highest-leverage activity in negotiation happens BEFORE
the conversation. Three quantities matter (Fisher + Ury 2011;
Raiffa 1982):

| Concept | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **BATNA** | Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement — what you do if the deal doesn't happen |
| **Reservation value** | The point at which you are indifferent between this deal and your BATNA — your walkaway number |
| **ZOPA** | Zone of Possible Agreement — the range between your reservation value and the counterparty's; deals exist only when ZOPA is non-empty |

Practical discipline:

- **Identify your BATNA explicitly before the meeting.** If your
  best alternative is "no deal and we miss the quarter," that's a
  bad BATNA — strengthen it (line up another vendor, prepare to go
  to a third party).
- **Estimate the counterparty's BATNA.** What's their walkaway?
  What pressure are they under?
- **Set your reservation value with explicit conditions.** Don't
  cross it in the heat of the moment.
- **Don't anchor on your BATNA** — your BATNA is a floor, not the
  number you should aim for.
- **Strengthen weak BATNAs.** A good investment of pre-meeting time
  is making sure you have a real alternative to walk to.

### Pattern 3: Interest-Based (Principled) Negotiation

Fisher + Ury + Patton's four prescriptions:

#### 1. Separate the people from the problem

Negotiations involve real people with emotions, perceptions, and
relationships. Attack the problem hard; treat the person with
respect. Mixing the two — attacking the person to weaken their
position — destroys both the deal and the relationship.

Tactics: explicit acknowledgment of the counterparty's perspective;
active listening; "I understand you see this as X" before
disagreeing; meta-conversation when the relationship is fraying.

#### 2. Focus on interests, not positions

A **position** is what you say you want ("$100K base, 0.5%
equity"). An **interest** is the underlying need ("I need to
support my family on this comp, and I need upside if this becomes
a $1B company").

Positions are stated; interests are inferred or asked about.
Multiple positions can satisfy the same interest — that's the
source of value-creating tradeoffs. Two positions can be
incompatible while their underlying interests are perfectly
compatible.

Always ask: "Why is that important to you?" and "What problem are
you trying to solve?" The answers reveal interests; the interests
reveal options.

#### 3. Invent options for mutual gain

Before deciding, **brainstorm options without commitment**.
Separate the inventing from the choosing. Common moves:

- Look for differences in valuation, timing, risk tolerance,
  forecasts, capabilities — they create tradeoffs.
- Bundle issues (price + payment terms + delivery date) instead of
  negotiating each in isolation.
- Add issues to the negotiation when there's nothing to trade.
- Look for differences in preference intensity — if you care
  intensely about A and they care intensely about B, trade.

#### 4. Insist on objective criteria

When stuck on a number, propose an **external standard**: market
comparables, industry benchmarks, prior deals, expert evaluation,
auction price. Reframes "your number vs my number" into "what does
the market say."

This is especially powerful in salary + vendor pricing
conversations: "Levels.fyi says senior eng at a Series C in NYC is
$200-240K base; I'm asking for the midpoint."

### Pattern 4: Tactical Empathy + The Voss Methodology

Chris Voss's hostage-negotiator framework (Voss + Raz 2016)
focuses on getting the counterparty to feel understood — which
opens information flow + reduces emotional escalation.

#### Mirroring

Repeat the last 1-3 words of what the counterparty just said,
phrased as a question. Triggers them to elaborate without you
asking explicitly.

> Counterparty: "We just can't go above $50K on this engagement."
> You: "Can't go above $50K?"
> Counterparty: "Right, because our quarterly budget is X, and we've
> already committed to Y and Z…"

#### Labelling

Verbally identify the counterparty's emotion or position to
dissipate it. Format: "It seems like…" / "It sounds like…" /
"It looks like you're feeling…"

> "It seems like you're frustrated that this RFP timeline keeps
> slipping."

Labels — especially of negative emotions — defuse them rather than
amplifying them.

#### "No"-oriented questions

People feel safer saying "no" than "yes" — "yes" feels like
commitment. Frame questions to invite "no":

> Instead of: "Do you have time to talk?" (pressure to say yes)
> Use: "Is now a bad time?" (safe no)

#### Calibrated questions (open-ended "how" / "what")

Questions starting with "how" or "what" can't be answered with
yes/no and force the counterparty to think + share information:

- "How am I supposed to do that?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you face?"
- "What would need to be true for X to work?"
- "How will we know it's working?"

#### "That's right" vs "you're right"

When the counterparty says "you're right," they're often dismissing
you. "That's right" means they feel understood. Aim for "that's
right" by summarising their position so accurately they have to
agree.

#### Ackerman bargaining model

For distributive number-bargaining:

1. Set your target price.
2. Set your first offer at 65% of target.
3. Plan three concessions at 85% / 95% / 100% of target.
4. Use empathy + labels between each move.
5. End with a non-round number (e.g., 37,893) — signals careful
   calculation.
6. End with a non-monetary throw-in.

### Pattern 5: Anchoring + The First-Offer Question

The first specific number mentioned in a negotiation strongly
biases the outcome — a robust behavioural finding (Galinsky +
Mussweiler 2001, replicated in dozens of contexts).

**Should you make the first offer?**

- **Yes**, if you have good information about the ZOPA. An
  aggressive but defensible first offer pulls the deal toward your
  side.
- **No**, if you have poor information. The counterparty's first
  offer is informative; let them speak first.

If they anchor first, **don't counter from their anchor**. Reframe
the discussion (objective criteria, different metric, different
issue) before naming any number. If you counter directly, you're
implicitly accepting the anchor as the reference point.

**Counter-anchor with a strong opposing anchor**. Don't move from
their anchor to "splitting the difference" — that's anchoring
working as designed against you.

### Pattern 6: Multi-Issue Tradeoff Design

Single-issue negotiations (just price) are zero-sum. Multi-issue
negotiations create value through **differential valuation**: if
each side cares about different things, trades produce joint gains.

Practical process:

1. **List every issue** before the meeting — not just price. For
   a vendor deal: price, term, payment terms, SLA, exclusivity,
   IP, support, scope, change-request handling, renewal terms,
   termination clauses, MFN clauses, audit rights.
2. **Score your importance** of each issue (e.g., 0-100, summing
   to 100).
3. **Estimate the counterparty's importance** of each.
4. **Look for differences.** Where their importance > yours +
   yours is high on something they don't care about → trade.
5. **Package issues** in proposals instead of negotiating each in
   sequence. "Here's a package that addresses X, Y, Z — what do
   you think?"

This scoring discipline shifts the conversation from positional
to joint optimisation. Combined with MESO (multiple equivalent
simultaneous offers — Malhotra + Bazerman 2007), it surfaces
preferences quickly.

### Pattern 7: MESO — Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers

Present 2-4 offers, all equally valuable to you, that differ
across issues. Example for a vendor:

| Offer | Price | Term | Payment | Exclusivity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $100K | 1y | NET-30 | Non-exclusive |
| B | $85K | 2y | NET-60 | Non-exclusive |
| C | $75K | 2y | NET-30 | Region-exclusive |

The counterparty's choice reveals which issues they value most.
You then build the next iteration around that signal. Avoids the
"single offer rejected" trap.

### Pattern 8: Contingency Contracts (Resolve Disagreements About the Future)

When parties disagree about a future event ("the integration will
work" vs "the integration will fail"), a contingency contract
prices the disagreement instead of negotiating around it.

> Buyer thinks the SaaS migration will save $500K. Seller agrees but
> demands $400K. Buyer offers: $300K upfront + $200K when savings
> are independently audited at $400K+ in year 2.

The party with confidence in their forecast gets paid if it's
right; the other side is protected if it's wrong. The disagreement
becomes a tradeable parameter instead of a deal-killer (Malhotra +
Bazerman 2007).

### Pattern 9: Post-Settlement Settlement

After signing, propose: "Could we both do better?" Re-examine the
deal for unexplored joint gains, knowing each party can veto. Often
unlocks 5-15% incremental value because pre-signing competitive
dynamics gave way to post-signing alignment.

### Pattern 10: The Voss-style "How"

In hard negotiations, "How am I supposed to do that?" + label +
silence is shockingly effective:

> Counterparty: "Your price needs to come down 30%."
> You: "It sounds like you're under significant cost pressure from
> your CFO." (label)
> Counterparty: "Yes, exactly, and…" (gives information)
> You: "How am I supposed to do that?" (calibrated question)
> Counterparty: (proposes a tradeoff, structural change, or
> reveals flexibility)

The question puts the problem back on them without escalation.

### Pattern 11: Cross-Cultural Negotiation

Hofstede's dimensions + Brett 2014 + Meyer 2014 inform style:

| Dimension | High-end behaviour | Low-end behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power Distance | Hierarchy respected; defer to senior; decisions slow + escalated | Egalitarian; junior can challenge; decisions distributed |
| Individualism | Direct negotiation, fast decisions, individual reward | Group consensus required, longer cycles, relationship-first |
| Uncertainty Avoidance | Detailed contracts, explicit clauses, slow trust | Looser contracts, faster trust, more reliance on relationship |
| Communication style (Meyer) | Low-context: explicit, direct, "say what you mean" | High-context: implicit, indirect, much is inferred |
| Disagreement style (Meyer) | Confrontational: open disagreement is fine | Confrontation-avoiding: open disagreement is rude |

Examples (each is a tendency, not a rule):

- US: low-context, confrontational, individualist, fast-deal
- Germany: low-context, confrontational, structured + detailed
- Japan: high-context, confrontation-avoiding, group-decision,
  long relationship-first
- China: high-context, hierarchical, long-term, relationship-first
- Brazil: high-context, confrontation-avoiding (with humour),
  relationship-first
- France: low-context but disagreement is intellectual sport;
  principles-first persuasion

The principal-level move is to **research the counterparty's
culture** + **adapt pace, formality, and directness** while
keeping your interests intact.

### Pattern 12: Negotiation Ethics — What's Never Acceptable

Negotiation involves selective disclosure, strategic framing, and
tactical silence. It does NOT involve:

- **Misrepresenting material facts.** Lying about something the
  other side would reasonably rely on (your financial condition,
  the product's specifications, your authority to make the deal,
  your alternatives).
- **Bribery, threats of violence, blackmail.**
- **Bad-faith negotiation** — pretending to want a deal you have
  no intention of signing.
- **Misrepresenting authority to bind.** If you can't sign without
  your board, say so before the conversation begins.

The Shell (2018) bright-line test: **Would a reasonable juror
consider this fraud?** If yes, don't do it. Strategic ambiguity
about your bottom line is fine; lying about the price your
competitor offered is not.

## Anti-Patterns

### Anti-Pattern 1: Positional Bargaining as Default

Trading positions ("I want $100, you want $50, let's meet at
$75") without exploring why each side wants what they want. The
$75 is rarely the value-maximising outcome — there are usually
multi-issue tradeoffs that produce $90 for both sides.

**Correction**: ask why. Ask about interests. Look for tradeoffs.
Reserve positional bargaining for the final phase of distributive
allocation, AFTER value creation is exhausted.

### Anti-Pattern 2: No BATNA Preparation

Walking into a negotiation without knowing your reservation value
or having a real alternative. Result: caving under pressure, or
walking away from deals that were actually better than your
alternative.

**Correction**: ALWAYS know your BATNA + reservation value before
the conversation. Write them down. Stick to them.

### Anti-Pattern 3: Win-at-All-Costs

Treating every negotiation as zero-sum + adversarial. Destroys
value creation, signals to the counterparty that they should also
defect, and produces fragile agreements the counterparty
underperforms on out of resentment.

**Correction**: distinguish single-shot transactions (where some
distributive intensity is fine) from repeated games (where joint
value creation matters more than per-deal capture).

### Anti-Pattern 4: Splitting the Difference Reflexively

When two anchors are on the table, defaulting to the midpoint.
This is anchoring working as designed — the side with the more
aggressive anchor wins.

**Correction**: refuse the midpoint anchor. Reframe (objective
criteria, different metric, package). Make a counter-anchor of
your own before any movement.

### Anti-Pattern 5: Premature Concessions

Conceding before the counterparty makes any movement, or conceding
without naming what you want in return ("OK, I'll drop the price"
without "in exchange for…"). Trains the counterparty that
concessions are free.

**Correction**: every concession is conditional + paired. "If you
can agree to X, I can move to Y." This signals you're not a soft
target + creates the tradeoff structure.

### Anti-Pattern 6: Confusing Position with Interest

Hearing "I need $150K base" and assuming the interest is the
$150K. Often the interest is "I need to support my mortgage" or "I
need to feel valued vs my prior offer." Multiple positions satisfy
each — sign-on bonus, equity, faster vesting, title.

**Correction**: ALWAYS probe for interest. "Why is that specific
number important?" "What problem is that solving?"

### Anti-Pattern 7: Failing to Walk Away

Sticking with a deal that's below your reservation value because
of sunk-cost (the meetings already invested) or fear (the deal
might collapse). The walkaway point exists precisely for the
moment you don't want to use it.

**Correction**: a strong BATNA + a pre-committed reservation value

- permission to walk. Walking away is sometimes the value-
maximising move.

### Anti-Pattern 8: Cultural Tone-Deafness

Bringing US-style fast, direct, confrontational bargaining to a
Japan, China, or Brazil counterparty. Or expecting a 6-month
relationship build before discussing terms with a New York
counterparty. Both produce ruptured deals.

**Correction**: research the counterparty's culture. Adapt pace,
formality, communication style, decision-cycle expectations.

### Anti-Pattern 9: Single-Issue Tunnel Vision

Negotiating only the headline number (price, salary, valuation)
when there are 10+ other negotiable issues that the counterparty
might value more or less than you. Result: you discover a deal
that gives them what they want at significant cost to you, when
trades were available.

**Correction**: prepare the full issue list BEFORE the meeting.
Score importance. Look for tradeoffs.

### Anti-Pattern 10: Treating Tactics as the Whole Game

Memorising Voss's mirroring + labels + calibrated questions and
applying them as a script. The counterparty notices. Tactics work
ONLY in service of genuine listening + genuine value creation.

**Correction**: tactics are the surface; preparation +
interest-discovery + tradeoff design are the substance. Use
tactics to surface information; use information to design value.

### Anti-Pattern 11: Lying

The temptation: misrepresent your BATNA ("I have another offer at
$150K" when you don't), misrepresent your authority, misrepresent
material facts. The professional cost when caught: catastrophic.
The personal cost: corrosion of judgement.

**Correction**: strategic silence + selective disclosure are
fine. Material lies are not. If you would be embarrassed to have
the conversation read aloud in court, don't say it.

### Anti-Pattern 12: Negotiating with the Wrong Person

Spending months negotiating terms with someone who lacks authority
to bind. Result: a "deal" that the actual decider rejects + a
re-negotiation from the new starting point.

**Correction**: early in the conversation: "Who else needs to be
involved for this to move forward? Do you have authority to bind?"

### Anti-Pattern 13: Ignoring Internal Negotiations

Treating internal cross-team conversations (resourcing, ownership
boundaries, roadmap priorities) as if they weren't negotiations.
Result: dominant teams get their way; subordinate teams accumulate
resentment that surfaces later as quit threats, knowledge silos,
or political resistance.

**Correction**: internal negotiations DESERVE the same preparation
as external. BATNA, interests, multi-issue tradeoffs, durable
agreements. Often more so — the relationship continues forever.

### Anti-Pattern 14: Conflating Empathy with Concession

Acknowledging the counterparty's perspective ("I see why this is
hard for you") and treating that as a substantive concession.
Empathy is free — it doesn't move the deal. Substantive
concessions are paid.

**Correction**: empathise generously (it's the cheap part);
concede precisely (it's the expensive part).

## Verification Checklist

Before declaring a negotiation methodology principal-level,
verify ALL the following:

### Preparation

- [ ] BATNA identified explicitly + (if weak) strengthened
- [ ] Reservation value set quantitatively with conditions
- [ ] Counterparty BATNA estimated + their pressure understood
- [ ] Full issue list compiled (not just price)
- [ ] Importance score assigned per issue (your side + estimate of theirs)
- [ ] Tradeoff candidates identified (issues you value differently)
- [ ] Authority of the person across the table confirmed
- [ ] Cultural research done if cross-border

### Process

- [ ] First offer decision made consciously (you anchor OR you
      let them anchor)
- [ ] Interests probed before commitment to any number
- [ ] Calibrated questions used to surface counterparty interests
- [ ] Labels + mirrors used to defuse emotional escalation
- [ ] MESO presented when issue-importance estimates are uncertain
- [ ] Objective criteria invoked when stuck on a number
- [ ] Every concession paired with a counter-ask
- [ ] Reservation value held when crossed

### Substantive outcome

- [ ] Joint value created (counterparty also got real value),
      not just claimed
- [ ] Agreement is durable (both sides will perform)
- [ ] Implementation mechanics specified (not just principles)
- [ ] Termination + exit terms clear
- [ ] Renegotiation triggers identified
- [ ] Written agreement reviewed by legal where applicable
- [ ] Post-settlement settlement check done

### Ethics

- [ ] No material misrepresentations made
- [ ] No fabricated alternatives quoted
- [ ] Authority + limitations disclosed before signing

## Why This Skill Exists

Negotiation appears in every senior-level role. Most people learn
it informally — by watching others, often badly. The cost of bad
negotiation:

- **Vendor deals** routinely leave 10-30% on the table by
  positional bargaining on price + ignoring tradeable issues.
- **Salary negotiations** cost engineers $50K-$200K over a career
  through under-asking + accepting first offers.
- **Internal resource allocations** turn into power games when
  framed as zero-sum, producing political residue that lasts
  years.
- **M&A + investor negotiations** are massively asymmetric: the
  other side has done thousands of these; first-time founders /
  acquirers learn at $50M per lesson.
- **Cross-cultural deals** collapse from style mismatch when the
  underlying interests were perfectly aligned.

Principal-level negotiation is a learnable craft. The frameworks
above — Harvard Method, BATNA discipline, multi-issue tradeoffs,
tactical empathy, contingency contracts, MESO — are taught at
business + law schools because they reliably produce better
outcomes.

This skill should fire when:

- A vendor contract is being negotiated (every clause is
  negotiable, including ones the vendor claims are "standard")
- A hiring offer is being constructed (multiple-issue trade with
  the candidate)
- A salary negotiation is happening (the employee deserves
  preparation discipline equal to the company's)
- Internal teams are arguing about ownership / priorities /
  resources
- An RFP / RFP-response is being prepared
- A board / investor conversation is being prepared
- A dispute is being resolved without litigation
- A partnership or JV is being structured

The principal-level response is to slow down, identify your BATNA

- reservation value, list the full issue set, probe the
counterparty's interests, look for trades, and reach durable
agreements both sides will perform on.

That's the entire purpose of this skill.

## Learning hooks

Per `~/.claude/rules/common/continuous-learning-mandate.md`:

**Signals to watch**:

- Negotiation entered without documented BATNA / reservation point / ZOPA (positional-bargaining trap looming)
- Single-issue framing where multi-issue tradeoffs (logrolling) would generate value
- "Win" measured as concession-count rather than joint-value created (Harvard Method weakening)
- Tactical empathy / labeling skipped on emotionally-loaded counterparts (Voss patterns dormant)
- Cross-cultural negotiation entered without context (high/low context, monochronic/polychronic mismatch)
- Anchor accepted without counter-anchor + rationale (negotiation analytics gap)
- Post-mortem skipped — wins + losses not catalogued for future thesis refinement
- Concession given without reciprocity ask (one-way value transfer)

**Refinement candidates**:

- New tactical pattern when a recurring counterparty type (procurement, GC, regulator) surfaces a new playbook
- Cross-cultural addendum when negotiation spans a previously-untouched cultural context
- Update to BATNA framework when market conditions shift (e.g., vendor supply collapse changes leverage math)
- New anti-pattern row when a negotiation failure traces to a missed signal documented in Fisher/Ury or Voss but absent here
