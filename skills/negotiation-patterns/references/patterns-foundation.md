## Pattern 1: The Two Dimensions — Creating + Claiming Value

Every negotiation has two simultaneous dynamics (Lax + Sebenius
1986):

1. **Creating value (integrative)** — finding tradeoffs across
   multiple issues so the joint outcome exceeds simple
   split-the-difference; the proverbial "expanding the pie"
2. **Claiming value (distributive)** — capturing as much of the
   value as possible for your side

These ARE in tension: aggressive claiming (extreme first offers,
threats, deception) signals competition and shuts down the
information sharing that creating value requires. Disclosing your
interests transparently invites the counterparty to share theirs
— but also lets them claim more.

The principal-level skill is calibrating: create enough value to
make the deal worth doing for both sides, then claim a fair share
of the surplus. Pure creators leave money on the table; pure
claimers either kill deals or build agreements that the
counterparty resents and underperforms.

## Pattern 2: BATNA, Reservation Value, ZOPA — the Preparation Triad

The single highest-leverage activity in negotiation happens BEFORE
the conversation. Three quantities matter (Fisher + Ury 2011;
Raiffa 1982):

| Concept | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **BATNA** | Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement — what you do if the deal doesn't happen |
| **Reservation value** | The point at which you are indifferent between this deal and your BATNA — your walkaway number |
| **ZOPA** | Zone of Possible Agreement — the range between your reservation value and the counterparty's; deals exist only when ZOPA is non-empty |

Practical discipline:

- **Identify your BATNA explicitly before the meeting.** If your
  best alternative is "no deal and we miss the quarter," that's a
  bad BATNA — strengthen it (line up another vendor, prepare to go
  to a third party).
- **Estimate the counterparty's BATNA.** What's their walkaway?
  What pressure are they under?
- **Set your reservation value with explicit conditions.** Don't
  cross it in the heat of the moment.
- **Don't anchor on your BATNA** — your BATNA is a floor, not the
  number you should aim for.
- **Strengthen weak BATNAs.** A good investment of pre-meeting time
  is making sure you have a real alternative to walk to.

## Pattern 3: Interest-Based (Principled) Negotiation

Fisher + Ury + Patton's four prescriptions:

### 1. Separate the people from the problem

Negotiations involve real people with emotions, perceptions, and
relationships. Attack the problem hard; treat the person with
respect. Mixing the two — attacking the person to weaken their
position — destroys both the deal and the relationship.

Tactics: explicit acknowledgment of the counterparty's perspective;
active listening; "I understand you see this as X" before
disagreeing; meta-conversation when the relationship is fraying.

### 2. Focus on interests, not positions

A **position** is what you say you want ("$100K base, 0.5%
equity"). An **interest** is the underlying need ("I need to
support my family on this comp, and I need upside if this becomes
a $1B company").

Positions are stated; interests are inferred or asked about.
Multiple positions can satisfy the same interest — that's the
source of value-creating tradeoffs. Two positions can be
incompatible while their underlying interests are perfectly
compatible.

Always ask: "Why is that important to you?" and "What problem are
you trying to solve?" The answers reveal interests; the interests
reveal options.

### 3. Invent options for mutual gain

Before deciding, **brainstorm options without commitment**.
Separate the inventing from the choosing. Common moves:

- Look for differences in valuation, timing, risk tolerance,
  forecasts, capabilities — they create tradeoffs.
- Bundle issues (price + payment terms + delivery date) instead of
  negotiating each in isolation.
- Add issues to the negotiation when there's nothing to trade.
- Look for differences in preference intensity — if you care
  intensely about A and they care intensely about B, trade.

### 4. Insist on objective criteria

When stuck on a number, propose an **external standard**: market
comparables, industry benchmarks, prior deals, expert evaluation,
auction price. Reframes "your number vs my number" into "what does
the market say."

This is especially powerful in salary + vendor pricing
conversations: "Levels.fyi says senior eng at a Series C in NYC is
$200-240K base; I'm asking for the midpoint."
