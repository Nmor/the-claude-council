## Pattern 4: Tactical Empathy + The Voss Methodology

Chris Voss's hostage-negotiator framework (Voss + Raz 2016)
focuses on getting the counterparty to feel understood — which
opens information flow + reduces emotional escalation.

### Mirroring

Repeat the last 1-3 words of what the counterparty just said,
phrased as a question. Triggers them to elaborate without you
asking explicitly.

> Counterparty: "We just can't go above $50K on this engagement."
> You: "Can't go above $50K?"
> Counterparty: "Right, because our quarterly budget is X, and we've
> already committed to Y and Z…"

### Labelling

Verbally identify the counterparty's emotion or position to
dissipate it. Format: "It seems like…" / "It sounds like…" /
"It looks like you're feeling…"

> "It seems like you're frustrated that this RFP timeline keeps
> slipping."

Labels — especially of negative emotions — defuse them rather than
amplifying them.

### "No"-oriented questions

People feel safer saying "no" than "yes" — "yes" feels like
commitment. Frame questions to invite "no":

> Instead of: "Do you have time to talk?" (pressure to say yes)
> Use: "Is now a bad time?" (safe no)

### Calibrated questions (open-ended "how" / "what")

Questions starting with "how" or "what" can't be answered with
yes/no and force the counterparty to think + share information:

- "How am I supposed to do that?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you face?"
- "What would need to be true for X to work?"
- "How will we know it's working?"

### "That's right" vs "you're right"

When the counterparty says "you're right," they're often dismissing
you. "That's right" means they feel understood. Aim for "that's
right" by summarising their position so accurately they have to
agree.

### Ackerman bargaining model

For distributive number-bargaining:

1. Set your target price.
2. Set your first offer at 65% of target.
3. Plan three concessions at 85% / 95% / 100% of target.
4. Use empathy + labels between each move.
5. End with a non-round number (e.g., 37,893) — signals careful
   calculation.
6. End with a non-monetary throw-in.

## Pattern 5: Anchoring + The First-Offer Question

The first specific number mentioned in a negotiation strongly
biases the outcome — a robust behavioural finding (Galinsky +
Mussweiler 2001, replicated in dozens of contexts).

**Should you make the first offer?**

- **Yes**, if you have good information about the ZOPA. An
  aggressive but defensible first offer pulls the deal toward your
  side.
- **No**, if you have poor information. The counterparty's first
  offer is informative; let them speak first.

If they anchor first, **don't counter from their anchor**. Reframe
the discussion (objective criteria, different metric, different
issue) before naming any number. If you counter directly, you're
implicitly accepting the anchor as the reference point.

**Counter-anchor with a strong opposing anchor**. Don't move from
their anchor to "splitting the difference" — that's anchoring
working as designed against you.

## Pattern 10: The Voss-style "How"

In hard negotiations, "How am I supposed to do that?" + label +
silence is shockingly effective:

> Counterparty: "Your price needs to come down 30%."
> You: "It sounds like you're under significant cost pressure from
> your CFO." (label)
> Counterparty: "Yes, exactly, and…" (gives information)
> You: "How am I supposed to do that?" (calibrated question)
> Counterparty: (proposes a tradeoff, structural change, or
> reveals flexibility)

The question puts the problem back on them without escalation.
