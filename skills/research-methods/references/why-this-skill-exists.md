## Why This Skill Exists

Research is the discipline that grounds claims in evidence.
Without it, organisations make decisions based on the loudest
voice in the room, the boss's prior, or the most cited tweet —
none of which correlate with truth.

The cost of bad research:

- **Strategic mistakes** built on "research" that was actually
  one person's hot take
- **Wasted investment** in pursuits the evidence had already
  ruled out
- **Customer harm** from products built on assumed-but-untested
  needs
- **Credibility loss** when a load-bearing claim turns out to
  be from a retracted study
- **Replication crisis costs** — billions spent on follow-on
  research from findings that never replicated
- **Legal exposure** when "research-backed" marketing claims
  fail FTC / regulatory scrutiny
- **Bad policy** when policymakers cite single studies as
  "the science says"

Principal-level research is a learnable craft. The frameworks
above — Booth's Craft of Research, Creswell's design textbook,
PRISMA for systematic reviews, Dillman for surveys, Braun +
Clarke for thematic analysis, Cochrane for evidence synthesis,
Ioannidis on replication, FAIR for data — are the canonical
references.

This skill should fire when:

- A literature review is being done
- A market sizing or competitive analysis is being prepared
- A survey or interview study is being designed
- An A/B test result is being interpreted
- A white paper or policy brief is being drafted
- A vendor's marketing claim is being verified
- Due diligence on an investment, vendor, or hire is happening
- A decision rests on "the research shows" — and we need to
  verify what the research actually shows
- A statistical claim needs to be unpacked
- A regulatory filing or compliance document is being drafted
- Conflicting studies need synthesis

The principal-level response is to ask the question precisely,
trace primary sources, pick methods fit for purpose, identify +
mitigate bias, report with statistical discipline + replication
context, and produce evidence that another researcher could
re-derive.

That's the entire purpose of this skill.

Standards cited grounding (verbatim, with editions):

- Booth + Colomb + Williams + Bizup + FitzGerald, *The Craft
  of Research* (4th ed 2016) — canonical research-design text.
- Creswell + Creswell, *Research Design* (6th ed 2023) — three
  paradigms.
- Maxwell, *Qualitative Research Design* (3rd ed 2013) —
  interactive design.
- Yin, *Case Study Research* (6th ed 2017) — case-study
  methodology.
- Glaser + Strauss, *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* (1967) —
  grounded theory.
- Braun + Clarke, "Using thematic analysis in psychology"
  (2006) — canonical thematic-analysis paper.
- Dillman + Smyth + Christian, *Internet, Phone, Mail, and
  Mixed-Mode Surveys* (4th ed 2014) — survey methodology.
- Cochrane Handbook (6.4, 2023) — systematic reviews.
- PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., BMJ 2021) — SR reporting.
- Ioannidis, "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"
  (2005) — replication crisis.
- Open Science Collaboration (Science 2015) — Many Labs
  replication.
- Pearl, *Causality* (2nd ed 2009) + *The Book of Why* (2018)
  — causal inference.
- Angrist + Pischke, *Mostly Harmless Econometrics* (2008) +
  *Mastering Metrics* (2014) — applied causal inference.
- Gelman + Hill + Vehtari, *Regression and Other Stories*
  (2020) — modern regression.
- Belmont Report (1979) — research ethics foundation.
- Declaration of Helsinki (1964, amended 2013) — medical
  research ethics.
- ICMJE Recommendations (2019) — authorship + reporting.
- FAIR Principles (Wilkinson et al., 2016) — data management.
- OECD Frascati Manual (2015) — R&D measurement.

These are the principal-level references a research practitioner
uses to ground methodology in evidence rather than opinion.
